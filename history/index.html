The history of Vaal Marina

The town is only a few decades old. The story of the ground it stands on runs from a river crossing with a sad name, through one of the great public works of the 1930s, to airliners landing on the water.

Before 1880

A river through the grassland

For most of its history this was not a shoreline at all. The Vaal was a Highveld river winding through open grassland, and it marked a border: the Transvaal on the north bank, the Orange Free State on the south.

People lived along it long before either republic existed. Sotho-Tswana communities settled on the banks of the Vaal, and the ruins of their stone dwellings can still be seen across the water at Deneysville. Artefacts were collected from the valley floor before it was flooded.

By the 1800s the land where Vaal Marina now stands was part of a farm called Koppiesfontein, a name that means the spring among the little hills. In the 1880s it was farmed by Hermanus Lombard, a man so strong that he was known as Sterk Hermanus and was said never to have been thrown in a wrestling match.

Below the farm, wagons crossed the river at a drift. Maps drawn between 1879 and about 1890 name it Luuise's Drift, Luise's Drift and Luces Drift; people remembered it as Lies Drift. The writer Eugène Marais recorded a story about how it may have earned the name: a bride travelling from the Free State to her wedding on the Transvaal side tried to cross when the river was running too strongly, and her wagon and six horses were swept away while the groom and guests watched from the bank.

1880

The night at Lies Drift

In December 1880 the Transvaal rose against British rule. At Bronkhorstspruit on 20 December a British column was stopped by Boer forces and badly beaten. Captain John Mitchell Elliot, paymaster of the 94th Regiment, was taken prisoner. So, separately, was a Captain Lambert.

The two officers were offered release on parole, on condition that they left the Transvaal by the shortest route and took no further part in the war. They accepted, and were sent south with a cart, two horses and an escort. When they reached the Vaal it was in flood. They would not abandon the cart to cross by boat, so the escort left them, and they wandered upstream looking for a way over. At Koppiesfontein, Hermanus Lombard gave them a meal and helped repair their cart.

Some days later they were found still inside the Transvaal and brought back to the farm. A commandant arrived at six in the evening and ordered them to Lies Drift at once. They reached the river after sunset, the current was strong, and the cart stuck fast. Lombard offered to lead the horses through and swim back. He was refused. Shots were fired, and Elliot was hit in the back and died instantly. Lambert jumped into the river and swam to the Free State bank. The date on Elliot's gravestone is the night of 29 December 1880.

The killing was raised at the peace talks that ended the war. Seven of the eight men of the escort stood trial in July 1881, and the court could not establish who fired the shot. Lombard did not appear. He left for Bechuanaland, was declared an outlaw, and spent decades in the Kalahari as a big-game hunter. In 1927, by then an old man, he gave his account to Eugène Marais for The Star and denied any guilt. He died in 1930, aged 91.

The drift itself now lies deep under the dam. One local historian places it on a line from the end of Sailfish Street across to Bultkop on the Free State side. The grave of Hermanus's younger brother, Barent Jacobus Lombard (1844 to 1933), and the ruin of a farmhouse still stand on part of Koppiesfontein, between the town and the R54.

1933 to 1938

A dam built in the Depression

Johannesburg has no large river of its own. As the Witwatersrand's mines and factories grew, the Rand Water Board built a barrage on the Vaal in the early 1920s, and within ten years it was no longer enough.

The answer was a far larger dam just below the point where the Wilge River joins the Vaal. The government and the Rand Water Board shared the scheme: about half the water was meant for irrigation at Vaalharts in the Northern Cape and the rest for the Witwatersrand. Work began in 1933, in the depths of the Great Depression, as part of a public works programme to create jobs.

About 800 men worked on the wall. In keeping with the labour policies of the time the scheme was first reserved for unemployed white men, unmarried and aged 18 to 45, who earned two shillings a day plus a bonus of one shilling and sixpence that was paid into a Post Office savings account and could only be drawn when they left. From 1935, as the economy recovered and men drifted away to better work, black workers were employed on parts of the dam as well.

What they built was a concrete gravity wall more than 500 metres long, with a 1.9 kilometre earth embankment on the Transvaal bank. It was called the Vaalbank Dam at first. On 13 December 1938 it overflowed for the first time, holding 994 million cubic metres of water.

Behind the wall the river spread out over drifts, farm roads and grazing. The valley below Koppiesfontein filled, and Lies Drift disappeared under the water. Across the water a new village had already been laid out in 1936 and named Deneysville, after Deneys Reitz.

1948

Flying boats on the Highveld

For a few years after the Second World War, one way to travel from England to Johannesburg ended on this water. On 4 May 1948 the British Overseas Airways Corporation began flying Short Solent flying boats between Southampton and the Vaal Dam, with passengers coming ashore at Deneysville.

The service did not last long, but it left the dam with one of the more unlikely chapters in South African aviation: an international airport with no runway, about 1,500 metres above sea level and hundreds of kilometres from the coast.

1952 to 1985

The water rises twice more

The dam was soon too small for the country it supplied. Industry was growing and the Free State goldfields were opening, so between 1952 and 1956 the wall was raised by 6.1 metres and fitted with 60 crest gates. Storage more than doubled.

A second raising of just over three metres was completed in 1985, most of it to hold back floods. Each time, the waterline climbed a little further up the slopes of the old farm.

Today the Vaal Dam holds about 2.6 billion cubic metres. It covers roughly 320 square kilometres, has more than 800 kilometres of shoreline, and touches three provinces: Gauteng, the Free State and Mpumalanga. Water from the Lesotho Highlands and from Sterkfontein Dam in the Drakensberg is fed into it to keep Gauteng supplied.

1970s and 1980s

A town for people who love the water

Vaal Marina is young, and its early records are thin. One local history writer puts its beginnings in the 1970s; a community account says it was proclaimed a holiday township in the mid-1980s. Both agree on why it was built: for people from the city who wanted a second home near the water, somewhere that might one day become the place they retired to.

The street map still says what they came for. Most of the shops are on Anchovy Road, and around it run Bass, Kob, Leervis, Harder, Haring and Sailfish.

A communal waterfront gave owners without their own frontage a place to launch. A yacht club, Pennant Nine, made its home on Sailfish Street. Resorts, a pub, a hardware shop and estate agents followed, and in time a secure marina and golf estate, Harbour Town, was built along Ring Road.

If you were here in the early years, we would like to hear from you. Photographs, plot plans, club newsletters and memories would all help us fill in this chapter.

1996

The flood of 1996

In February 1996 the largest flood ever recorded at the dam arrived when it was already full. More than 4,700 cubic metres of water a second poured in. Engineers held the outflow to about half of that to protect the towns downstream, and on 19 February storage peaked at 118.5 percent of full supply.

Between mid-December 1995 and mid-March 1996 enough water flowed into the Vaal Dam to fill it three times over. People who were here still measure other summers against it.

Today

Vaal Marina now

Vaal Marina is part of the Midvaal Local Municipality in Gauteng's Sedibeng District, and one of three villages on the dam, with Deneysville and Oranjeville. The 2011 census counted 701 residents across about 25 square kilometres, which now takes in the areas of Aloe Fjord, Vaal La Mer and Mamello as well as the original town.

On a Saturday the count is rather higher. Weekenders arrive from Johannesburg and the East Rand, boats go in at the slipways, and a parkrun sets off in the morning. More people are also choosing to live here all year and work from home.

The sailing calendar on the dam includes the Round the Island Race, sailed around the five-kilometre island in the middle of the dam and recognised by Guinness World Records as the largest inland yacht race, with 389 boats finishing.
